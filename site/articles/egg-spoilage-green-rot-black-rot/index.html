    Green Rot, Black Rot: Egg Spoilage by Bacteria

    Not all egg spoilage looks or smells the same. “Green rot” and “black rot” are distinct spoilage syndromes caused by different bacteria, producing different visual changes, odors, and food safety risks. Understanding egg spoilage by bacteria is essential for quality control in egg production and processing.

    Pseudomonas Fluorescens: Green Rot

    Imagine cracking an egg into a bowl and noticing the white has a faint greenish tinge, like pond water in summer. It smells odd — not the rotten-egg stench you’d expect, but something fruity, almost floral. That’s green rot. Caused by Pseudomonas fluorescens , a bacterium that thrives at refrigerator temperatures (4-10°C) as if it were lounging in a heated spa.

    Pseudomonas produces a pigment called pyoverdine , which glows greenish-yellow under UV light — like the fluorescent dye in a highlighter pen. In normal kitchen light, the infected egg white looks slightly grayish or greenish, like diluted antifreeze. The yolk may still look intact, but the albumen has begun to liquefy.

    Real-world scene: A commercial egg-breaking facility runs thousands of eggs through an automated candling line. A batch with green rot gets flagged only after broken-out inspection. The fruity odor masks the spoilage — workers don’t immediately recognize it as bad. This is why candling alone isn’t enough; processors rely on UV inspection to catch pyoverdine fluorescence before it reaches the pasteurizer.

    Proteus, Serratia, and Other Bacteria: Black Rot

    Black rot is the horror movie version of egg spoilage. Caused by Proteus vulgaris , Serratia marcescens , and related bacteria, it turns the egg into a dark, semi-liquid mass that smells like a swamp crossed with a sulfur spring. The classic “rotten egg” odor — hydrogen sulfide — comes from these bacteria digesting the egg’s proteins. The yolk turns black, reacting with the released iron to form ferrous sulfide (FeS), the same compound that makes ancient Roman coins look dark.

    Comparison: If green rot is like having a single moldy spot on a piece of bread, black rot is like finding an entire loaf turned into slime inside the bag. Green rot creeps in slowly; black rot explodes, especially if the egg was stored at room temperature or had a cracked shell.

    Case study — the warm truck incident: In 2019, a refrigerated truck carrying 24,000 eggs malfunctioned during a cross-country delivery. The internal temperature climbed to 28°C for 18 hours before the driver noticed. By the time the eggs arrived, inspectors found that over 12% of the eggs had developed black rot. The waste disposal cost exceeded the value of the product. This illustrates why cold chain integrity is non-negotiable — once proteolytic bacteria start breaking down egg proteins, the process accelerates exponentially.

    The Air Cell: A Gateway for Bacteria

    An egg is not a sealed fortress. It has 7,000 to 17,000 microscopic pores in its shell — like an old brick wall with hairline cracks everywhere. As the egg cools after being laid, contraction pulls air (and potentially bacteria) inward through these pores. This is called thermal pumping , and it’s the primary mechanism by which spoilage bacteria enter a clean, uncracked egg.

    The air cell at the wide end of the egg is the first landing zone for incoming bacteria. If the egg is fresh, the air cell is small and the internal membranes are intact. As the egg ages and the air cell grows, the membranes thin, making it easier for bacteria to cross into the albumen. Think of it as a castle wall that thins with age — eventually, the invaders break through.

    Practical Lessons for Consumers

    If an egg smells “fruity” when you crack it, that’s green rot — not a floral variety. Discard it even if it looks okay The float test cannot reliably distinguish between green rot and black rot. Both can sink or float depending on age A cracked egg is like an open door. Discard any egg with visible cracks, regardless of how fresh it seems Refrigeration slows both types of rot but doesn’t stop them entirely. Use eggs within 3-5 weeks of purchase If you crack an egg and see a dark yolk or black albumen, you don’t need to smell it — the visual is enough
