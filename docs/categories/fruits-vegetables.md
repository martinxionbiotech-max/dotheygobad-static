# Fruits & Vegetables

Fresh fruit and vegetables are alive after harvest. They respire, transpire and respond to ethylene, which means their shelf life is a biological process rather than a chemical countdown. A respiring product consumes oxygen, releases heat and loses water; how fast that happens depends on temperature, and how long it lasts depends on how much damage the tissue has already taken.

The dominant failure modes are mechanical and fungal. Bruising breaks cells, releasing juice that feeds Botrytis, Penicillium and Alternaria. Chilling injury — bananas below 13 °C, tomatoes below 10 °C — damages membranes and produces the pitting and browning that consumers read as spoilage. Water loss of 5–10% is enough to make produce unsaleable even when it is microbiologically sound.

The articles here cover those mechanisms per product: why avocados brown, why onions sprout, why lettuce wilts in the drawer and not on the counter.

## What drives shelf life here

- **Respiration rate** sets the clock; cooling is the single most effective intervention.
- **Ethylene** accelerates ripening and senescence — and one ripe banana affects everything in the drawer.
- **Chilling injury** happens above freezing, when tropical produce is stored too cold.
- **Water loss** of 5–10% causes shrivel and loss of saleable weight.
